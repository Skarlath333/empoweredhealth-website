Learning objectives

  • Order tests that will change management, and anticipate false positives.
  • Explain reference intervals and when "optimal" ranges are evidence-based.
  • Judge specialty tests by their evidence before ordering.
Lesson 1 of 3

Ordering with purpose

Before ordering, ask: will this result change what I do? If not, it adds cost, worry and follow-up testing.

  • Panels multiply false positives. Reference intervals cover 95% of healthy people, so each test flags about 1 in 20 healthy patients. With 20 independent tests, the chance of at least one "abnormal" result is about 64%.
  • Pretest probability matters. A positive result in a low-risk patient is more likely to be a false positive.
  • Cost is part of the decision. Many specialty tests are self-pay. Tell patients the cost and what the result could change.

Choosing Wisely examples that apply here: don't screen healthy adults for vitamin D deficiency, don't order reverse T3 routinely, and don't use IgG testing to diagnose food allergy or intolerance.

Lesson 2 of 3

Reference vs. optimal ranges

A reference interval is the middle 95% of results from a reference population. It describes what is common, not always what is healthy.

  • Some "optimal" targets are evidence-based: LDL goals by cardiovascular risk, A1c targets, ferritin of 75 ng/mL or higher for restless legs, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D of 20 ng/mL or higher for bone health (Institute of Medicine).
  • Many are not. Narrow "functional ranges" for TSH, fasting insulin or other markers often lack outcome data. Treating to them can cause harm, such as overtreating thyroid disease.
  • Results vary. Biological variation, lab methods, fasting, time of day (testosterone and cortisol), recent exercise, and biotin all change results. Repeat a surprising value before acting.
  • Trends beat single values. A rising A1c within the normal range tells you more than one result.

When a patient brings outside results, thank them, explain what the test can and can't show, and refocus on findings that change care.

Clinical pearls

  • Ask "What will I do differently with this result?" before ordering.
  • Repeat a surprising result before you treat it.
  • Most cardiometabolic risk can be estimated from a standard lipid panel, A1c, blood pressure, plus a one-time Lp(a).

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Critical values (very high potassium, very low sodium, very high calcium, severe anemia): act the same day.
  • A test result that doesn't fit the patient: consider lab error, interference (biotin) or specimen problems.
  • Incidental findings: follow evidence-based guidance and avoid cascades of further testing.

Case

A patient brings a $400 panel showing "high" IgG to eggs, dairy and wheat, a "low" salivary cortisol pattern, and an MTHFR variant. They've cut out eggs, dairy and wheat and feel worse.

Show discussion

Explain that IgG reflects exposure, not intolerance; the salivary panel isn't validated for adrenal fatigue; and MTHFR variants are common and don't change care. Help them reintroduce foods one at a time. If their symptoms suggest celiac disease, test once they are eating wheat again, since the test needs gluten exposure. Then run a focused evaluation of the symptoms that brought them in.

Patient handout · plain language

Understanding your labs

  • "Normal" ranges cover most healthy people. A result slightly outside the range is often fine, and we may repeat it.
  • More tests aren't always better. Each extra test raises the chance of a false alarm.
  • Before blood work: ask if you need to fast, stop biotin for 2 days, and tell us about supplements.
  • Some tests sold online aren't reliable, such as IgG food sensitivity tests. Ask us before paying for one.

Good questions to ask about any test: What will this show? Will it change my treatment? What does it cost?
