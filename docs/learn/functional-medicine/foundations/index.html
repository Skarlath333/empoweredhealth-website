Learning objectives

  • Take an extended history and timeline that links life events to symptom onset.
  • Explain where root-cause care adds to, and doesn't replace, guideline-based treatment.
Lesson 1 of 3

Systems thinking in practice

A diagnosis tells you what is happening. Root-cause care adds a second question: why in this patient, and why now? Two patients with stage 1 hypertension may share a label but not a cause. One has untreated sleep apnea, the other a high-sodium diet and heavy alcohol use. Treating the blood pressure is necessary for both. Finding the driver changes the rest of the plan.

Rather than reviewing organ systems in isolation, map how they interact. A useful working set of upstream domains:

DomainAsk aboutCommonly linked to
SleepDuration, snoring, witnessed apneas, shift workInsulin resistance, hypertension, mood, weight
NutritionUltra-processed food, protein, fiber, alcoholDyslipidemia, fatty liver, GI symptoms
MovementSteps, strength training, sitting timeGlycemic control, sarcopenia, mood
StressLoad, supports, trauma historySleep, GI function, blood pressure
ExposuresSmoking, occupation, water source, home ageRespiratory, endocrine and cancer risk
HistoryFamily history, medications, antibiotic and PPI useNutrient depletion, microbiome changes

The goal is a problem list where each diagnosis sits next to its likely contributors. That list becomes the plan: treat the diagnosis, and work on the two or three contributors most likely to move it.

Lesson 2 of 3

The extended intake and timeline

The extended intake has three parts, and it can be spread across two visits.

  1. The story. Start open-ended: "When did you last feel well?" Then ask what changed around that time. Patients often know the turning point (a move, a pregnancy, an infection, a new medication) even when they haven't connected it to their symptoms.
  2. The timeline. Lay out life events in order next to symptom onset: birth and early life, major infections and antibiotic courses, surgeries, pregnancies, medication starts and stops, losses and other stressors. Look for clusters.
  3. Validated screens. Use short, scored tools instead of open-ended review of systems where you can.

PHQ-9DepressionItem 9 asks about self-harm; have a plan for positive answers

Close the intake by agreeing on priorities. A patient with eight concerns does better with two or three changes they choose than with a twelve-item plan. Write those down together and set a follow-up date.

Lesson 3 of 3

Working alongside conventional care

Root-cause care is an addition to standard medicine, not a replacement for it. In practice that means:

  • Guidelines first. Screenings, vaccines, and evidence-based medications stay in the plan. Lifestyle work runs alongside treatment, not instead of it, unless the risk is low and the patient chooses a time-limited trial with a set recheck.
  • Shared decision-making. Present options with honest effect sizes. "Lifestyle change can lower blood pressure about as much as one medication" is more useful than "diet can fix this."
  • Supplements are medicines. Under U.S. law they are not reviewed by the FDA for safety or benefit before sale. Prefer products with third-party testing (USP Verified or NSF Certified), check interactions (St. John's wort induces CYP3A4; vitamin K affects warfarin), and record them on the medication list.
  • Coordinate. Send visit notes to the primary care clinician and specialists. A patient seeing several clinicians needs one shared plan.
  • Know your scope. Follow your state's scope-of-practice and collaboration rules, and current DEA rules for prescribing controlled substances (such as testosterone) by telehealth, which have been changing.

Evidence quality varies widely in this field. This course says when an idea is well supported, when it is promising, and when it is not supported. Patients deserve the same honesty.

Clinical pearls

  • Review the medication list as a possible cause. Long-term metformin can lower B12, PPIs can lower magnesium and B12, and statins are associated with muscle symptoms.
  • Ask "When did you last feel well?" early. The answer often points to the trigger.
  • Limit the plan to two or three changes per visit, chosen with the patient.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Unintentional weight loss, GI bleeding, new neurologic deficits, chest pain, or fevers and night sweats.
  • Overdue age-appropriate cancer screening. Get it done before attributing symptoms to lifestyle.
  • A positive answer on PHQ-9 item 9: assess suicide risk the same day; the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text.

Case

A 48-year-old with fatigue, an A1c of 6.0% and blood pressure of 138/88 sleeps 5.5 hours a night and snores loudly. Before adding medications, what would you screen for first?

Show discussion

Screen for obstructive sleep apnea (STOP-BANG, then a home sleep test or polysomnography if positive). Untreated sleep apnea worsens blood pressure, insulin resistance and fatigue, so treating it can improve all three. Address sleep duration at the same time, and treat blood pressure per guidelines if it stays elevated.

Patient handout · plain language

What is functional medicine?

Functional medicine asks why a health problem is happening. Your body's systems are connected. Poor sleep raises stress hormones, which affect blood sugar, which affects energy and mood. So we look at your food, sleep, movement, stress, environment and history, and work on those along with your symptoms.

It works alongside your regular medical care, not instead of it. You may still need medicines, screenings and specialists.

Try this week: For 3 days, write down what you ate, how you slept, and your energy from 1 to 10 each afternoon. Bring it to your next visit.

Tell us about every supplement you take. Some supplements change how medicines work.
