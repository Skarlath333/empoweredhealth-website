Lesson 1 of 4

Dietary patterns with evidence

Patterns beat single nutrients. The strongest outcome data support a few overlapping patterns built on vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish, with little ultra-processed food.

PatternKey evidenceBest fit
MediterraneanPREDIMED: about 30% fewer major cardiovascular events with added olive oil or nutsCardiovascular risk, metabolic syndrome
DASHLowered systolic pressure about 11 mmHg in people with hypertension; more with sodium reductionHypertension
Higher fiberTarget about 14 g per 1,000 kcal (roughly 25 to 38 g a day); linked to lower mortality and diabetes riskAlmost everyone
Less ultra-processed foodNIH inpatient trial: people ate about 500 kcal a day more on an ultra-processed dietWeight, appetite

Protein deserves attention in older adults and during weight loss. Expert groups suggest about 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults, spread across meals, to help preserve muscle.

Lesson 2 of 4

Elimination diets done safely

Elimination diets are useful tools with real risks: nutrient gaps, social burden, and disordered eating. Use them with a clear question and an end date.

  • Test for celiac disease first. Order tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) with total IgA while the patient is still eating gluten. Going gluten-free first can make the tests falsely negative.
  • Low-FODMAP has the best evidence for IBS. It runs in three phases: restriction for 2 to 6 weeks, structured reintroduction, then a personalized long-term diet. A dietitian improves results.
  • Skip IgG food sensitivity panels. IgG to foods reflects exposure, not intolerance. Allergy societies advise against using them, and they lead to needless restriction.
  • Be careful in children, pregnancy, and anyone with a history of eating disorders. Screen before you restrict.

A good elimination trial removes one or a few suspected foods for 2 to 4 weeks, tracks symptoms, then reintroduces one food at a time over 2 to 3 days each.

Lesson 3 of 4

Anti-inflammatory eating and weight

"Anti-inflammatory" is a loose label, but the foods behind it are well studied: fish twice a week, olive oil as the main fat, plenty of colorful plants, nuts and legumes, and fewer refined starches, sugary drinks and processed meats. These patterns lower CRP modestly and improve cardiometabolic markers.

Alcohol raises blood pressure, triglycerides and liver fat and worsens sleep. There is no level that is good for health; less is better.

Patients on GLP-1 medications eat much less, so food quality matters more. Prioritize protein at each meal, fiber and fluids for constipation, and resistance training at least twice a week to limit muscle loss.

Lesson 4 of 4

Counseling that sticks

Advice alone rarely changes behavior. Brief motivational interviewing helps. Use OARS:

  • Open questions: "What would you like to change about how you eat?"
  • Affirmations: notice effort, not just results.
  • Reflections: say back what you heard, including mixed feelings.
  • Summaries: end with the plan in the patient's words.

Turn the plan into one or two specific goals ("vegetables at lunch and dinner on weekdays") rather than rules ("eat clean"). Make it affordable: frozen vegetables, canned beans and fish, oats and eggs all count. Refer to a registered dietitian when you can; Medicare covers medical nutrition therapy for diabetes and kidney disease.

Clinical pearls

  • Order celiac serology before anyone goes gluten-free.
  • Ask what the patient ate yesterday. It gives a more honest picture than "How is your diet?"
  • Every elimination diet needs a reintroduction phase and an end date.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Unintentional weight loss, trouble swallowing, or persistent vomiting.
  • Signs of an eating disorder (restriction, bingeing, purging, fear of foods). Refer before prescribing any restrictive diet.
  • Iron deficiency anemia or chronic diarrhea that hasn't been worked up.

Case

A 34-year-old with bloating and loose stools stopped eating gluten two months ago and feels somewhat better. She asks if she has celiac disease.

Show discussion

Her current tTG-IgA may be falsely negative because she isn't eating gluten. Options are a gluten challenge (eating gluten daily for several weeks, following current guidance) before serology, or HLA-DQ2/DQ8 genetic testing, which can rule celiac disease out if negative. If celiac is excluded, consider IBS and a dietitian-guided low-FODMAP trial.

Patient handout · plain language

Eating to feel better

You don't need a perfect diet. Small steps add up:

  • Fill half your plate with vegetables or fruit. Frozen and canned count.
  • Add beans, lentils, nuts or whole grains for fiber.
  • Use olive oil as your main fat, and eat fish twice a week if you can.
  • Include protein (eggs, fish, chicken, beans, Greek yogurt) at each meal.
  • Cut back on sugary drinks, chips, and packaged snacks.

Thinking about cutting out gluten or other foods? Talk with us first. Some tests only work while you're still eating those foods.

Try this week: Pick one meal a day and add a vegetable to it.
