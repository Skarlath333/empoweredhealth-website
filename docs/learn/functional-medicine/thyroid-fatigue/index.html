Lesson 1 of 3

The fatigue workup

Most fatigue has more than one cause. Start with sleep, mood, medications, alcohol, menstrual blood loss, diet, and activity. A focused first round of labs:

TestLook forNotes
CBCAnemiaMicrocytosis suggests iron deficiency
FerritinIron deficiencyUnder 30 ng/mL indicates deficiency; ferritin rises with inflammation, so a normal value doesn't always rule it out
TSHThyroid dysfunctionAdd free T4 if abnormal
CMP and A1cKidney, liver, glucoseHyperglycemia causes fatigue
B12DeficiencyCheck with metformin, PPIs, vegan diet, or older age; add methylmalonic acid if 200 to 400 pg/mL
Pregnancy testPregnancyWhen applicable

Iron deficiency without anemia can cause fatigue: in one trial, oral iron improved fatigue in non-anemic women with ferritin under 50 ng/mL. Always ask why iron is low. Men and postmenopausal women with iron deficiency need a GI evaluation.

Vitamin D: the Endocrine Society (2024) advises against routine 25-hydroxyvitamin D testing in healthy adults. Test when there is a clinical reason, such as malabsorption, osteoporosis, or kidney disease.

Lesson 2 of 3

Reading thyroid labs

PatternLikely meaningNext step
High TSH, low free T4Overt hypothyroidismTreat; check TPO antibodies for Hashimoto's
High TSH, normal free T4Subclinical hypothyroidismRepeat in 6 to 12 weeks; treat if TSH 10 or more, pregnancy or trying to conceive, or consider with symptoms
Low TSH, high free T4 or T3HyperthyroidismTRAb or uptake scan; refer as needed
Low TSH, low free T4Possible central hypothyroidism or acute illnessEvaluate the pituitary
  • Biotin supplements can distort thyroid results. Have patients stop biotin at least 2 days before testing.
  • Older adults: TSH rises with age. In the TRUST trial, levothyroxine did not improve symptoms in adults 65 and older with subclinical hypothyroidism.
  • Reverse T3 is not recommended for routine use and doesn't guide treatment.
Lesson 3 of 3

Treating hypothyroidism well

  • Dose: full replacement is about 1.6 mcg/kg/day for younger healthy adults. Start 25 to 50 mcg a day in older adults or anyone with heart disease.
  • Absorption: take on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast (or at bedtime, consistently), and 4 hours apart from calcium, iron and some other medications. PPIs and celiac disease reduce absorption.
  • Monitoring: recheck TSH 6 to 8 weeks after any dose change, then yearly when stable.
  • Avoid overtreatment. A suppressed TSH raises the risk of atrial fibrillation and bone loss, especially in older and postmenopausal patients.
  • Combination T4/T3: guidelines don't recommend it routinely. A 2021 international consensus allows a trial in patients who still have symptoms on levothyroxine with a normal TSH, after other causes are addressed, with monitoring. Desiccated thyroid has a fixed, high T3 ratio and is not first-line.

If a patient with a normal TSH still feels exhausted, revisit the fatigue workup: sleep apnea, depression, iron, and life load are more often the answer than thyroid dosing.

Clinical pearls

  • Ask about biotin before every thyroid test.
  • Ferritin is an acute phase reactant. A "normal" ferritin with inflammation can hide iron deficiency; check transferrin saturation too.
  • Persistent fatigue with a normal TSH is rarely a thyroid problem. Screen sleep and mood.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Fatigue with weight loss, fevers, night sweats, or swollen lymph nodes.
  • Iron deficiency in men or postmenopausal women: refer for GI evaluation.
  • A thyroid nodule, rapid neck swelling, or hoarseness: ultrasound and referral.
  • Suspected myxedema or thyroid storm: emergency care.

Case

A 38-year-old woman with heavy periods reports exhaustion. Hemoglobin is 12.4 g/dL, ferritin 14 ng/mL, and TSH 2.1. She asks for a full thyroid panel with reverse T3.

Show discussion

She has iron deficiency without anemia, a likely cause of her fatigue. Her TSH is normal, and reverse T3 won't change management. Treat with oral iron (alternate-day dosing can improve absorption and tolerance), evaluate the heavy menstrual bleeding, and recheck ferritin in about 8 to 12 weeks. Screen sleep and mood as well.

Patient handout · plain language

Why am I so tired?

Feeling tired all the time is common, and it usually has more than one cause. We'll look at:

  • Sleep: how long, how well, and whether you snore
  • Blood work: iron, thyroid, blood sugar, and B12
  • Mood and stress: these affect energy a lot
  • Medicines and alcohol

Before your blood test: stop biotin (often in hair, skin and nail vitamins) for at least 2 days. It can make thyroid results wrong.

If you take thyroid medicine: take it on an empty stomach with water, and wait 4 hours before taking calcium or iron.

Try this week: Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, even on weekends.
