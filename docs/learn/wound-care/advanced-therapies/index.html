Lesson 1 of 2

Negative pressure wound therapy

NPWT removes drainage, reduces edema, draws wound edges together and promotes granulation. It fits deep wounds with moderate to heavy drainage, dehisced surgical wounds, and graft fixation.

Before starting: treat osteomyelitis and untreated infection, debride necrotic tissue (a practical rule of thumb is at least 60% granulation in the bed), and confirm no exposed vessels, untreated cancer in the wound, or unexplored fistula. Use caution with anticoagulation.

  1. Cut the foam to fit the wound bed, without overlapping intact skin.
  2. Windowpane the surrounding skin with strips of drape (or hydrocolloid) to prevent maceration.
  3. Place the foam, then cover with drape laid flat with no wrinkles or channels.
  4. Cut a small hole over the foam and apply the suction pad.
  5. Start at 125 mmHg continuous (common default), and check for a tight seal.
  6. Change about three times a week; check under tubing for device pressure injuries.

Single-use, portable NPWT systems suit smaller, lower-drainage wounds and closed incisions.

TherapyExamplesBest fit
Cellular and tissue-based products (skin substitutes)Bilayered living cell constructs, fish skin, dermal matricesDiabetic foot and venous ulcers that stall despite good standard care

Electrical stimulation, ultrasound mistPulsed current, low-frequency ultrasoundAdjuncts for stalled wounds and biofilm

Make them count. Advanced products fail on wounds with poor perfusion, uncontrolled infection, ongoing pressure or poor nutrition. Fix those first. Document at least 4 weeks of failed standard care with measurements, since payers and Medicare coverage policies for skin substitutes require it, and get prior authorization where needed. Coverage rules for skin substitutes are changing, so check current Medicare and payer policies before applying.

Clinical pearls

  • Windowpane the edges and get a flat, tight seal. Most NPWT failures are leaks.
  • Advanced products amplify good care. They don't replace offloading, perfusion and nutrition.
  • Measure and document weekly so you can show a stall before moving to advanced therapy.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Bleeding into the NPWT canister: stop therapy and evaluate immediately.
  • Untreated osteomyelitis or exposed vessels: NPWT is not appropriate.
  • Graft failure or infection after a skin substitute: reassess perfusion, infection and pressure.

Case

A clean, granulating stage 4 sacral wound (80% granulation, heavy drainage) has no signs of osteomyelitis on MRI. The patient is on a low air loss mattress and eating well.

Show discussion

A good NPWT candidate. Windowpane the edges, cut foam to the bed, seal flat, run 125 mmHg continuous, and change three times a week while keeping pressure off the sacrum. Track weekly measurements.

Patient handout · plain language

Advanced wound treatments

If a wound isn't healing with standard care, we may suggest:

  • A wound vac (negative pressure therapy): a small pump with a sealed dressing that pulls fluid out and helps new tissue grow. Keep the pump on and call us if it alarms and you can't fix the seal.
  • Skin substitutes or membranes: thin sheets placed on the wound to help it close. They may need insurance approval.
  • Skin grafts or hyperbaric oxygen for certain wounds.

These work best when pressure is off the wound, blood flow is good, and you're eating enough protein.

Call us right away if you see bright red blood in the wound vac tubing or canister.
