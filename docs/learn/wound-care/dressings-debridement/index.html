Lesson 1 of 3

The dry/wet, shallow/deep framework

Most wounds fit one of four boxes. The box tells you whether to add moisture, remove moisture, or keep things dry.

WoundGoalCommon choices
Dry shallow (stable eschar, minimal drainage)Keep dry and protectedPovidone-iodine or skin barrier film painted three to four times daily; leave stable eschar alone
Wet shallow (partial thickness, moderate to heavy drainage)Absorb drainage, protect skinAntimicrobial foam (for example Hydrofera Blue), silver alginate, hydrocolloid for light drainage; zinc barrier on surrounding skin
Dry deep (stage 3 or 4, little drainage)Add moisture, debride autolyticallyMedical-grade honey or hydrogel on gauze lightly packed, foam cover; collagen once clean
Wet deep (stage 3 or 4, heavy drainage)Control moisture and bioburden, then maintainQuarter- to half-strength Dakin's moistened gauze for 1 to 2 weeks, then silver alginate or antimicrobial foam; consider NPWT

Exception: in arterial wounds without adequate blood flow, and in frail or end-of-life patients, keep wounds dry with stable eschar rather than moist, even if deep.

Lesson 3 of 3

Debridement: when and how

MethodHowBest for
Sharp (conservative)Scalpel, curette or scissors by a trained providerFast removal of slough, callus and nonviable tissue; within scope and privileges
AutolyticMoisture-retentive dressings let the body's enzymes workSmall amounts of slough; patients who can't tolerate sharp debridement
EnzymaticCollagenase ointment (Santyl)Slough and soft eschar
MechanicalIrrigation, monofilament pads, ultrasonic mistLoose debris; avoid repeated wet-to-dry gauze, which is painful and nonselective
BiologicalSterile medical maggotsSelective debridement when other methods fail

Less is often more. Debride when there is loose necrotic tissue, slough, callus or biofilm and when it will help healing, not at every visit to make a wound look clean. Over-debridement removes healthy tissue, causes pain, and is a common reason wounds stall.

Don't debride stable, dry eschar on heels or in ischemic limbs, or wounds without adequate blood flow, until perfusion is addressed. Check anticoagulation and platelets before sharp debridement.

Clinical pearls

  • Ask two questions first: is it dry or wet, and is it shallow or deep?
  • Stable, dry eschar on a heel or an ischemic limb is a natural dressing. Leave it alone.
  • Pick a few products you know well and learn every facility's equivalents.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Pain, bleeding or exposed structures during debridement beyond what you expected: stop and reassess.
  • Eschar that becomes soft, boggy, draining or surrounded by redness: it's no longer stable; evaluate for infection.
  • Debriding a foot or leg wound with unknown perfusion: check the ABI first.

Case

A stage 4 sacral wound has heavy drainage, 50% slough, and macerated edges. The facility carries Dakin's, silver alginate, foam and zinc paste.

Show discussion

It's a wet deep wound. Start with Dakin's-moistened gauze and an absorptive cover once or twice daily for 1 to 2 weeks to reduce drainage and bioburden, protect the edges with zinc paste, and debride slough selectively. Then switch to silver alginate or antimicrobial foam and consider NPWT once the bed is clean and granulating. Fix the pressure and nutrition at the same time.

Patient handout · plain language

Caring for your dressing at home

  • Wash your hands before and after touching the dressing.
  • Change it only as often as we told you. Some dressings stay on for several days.
  • Keep it dry when you bathe unless we said otherwise.
  • If the dressing leaks or falls off, replace it the same day.
  • Don't put creams, peroxide or home remedies on the wound unless we recommend them.

A dark, dry scab is sometimes left on purpose. It can protect the wound underneath. Tell us if it gets soft, starts draining, or the skin around it turns red.
