Lesson 1 of 3

Measuring and describing wounds

  • Measure in centimeters: length (head to toe) by width (side to side, perpendicular) by depth (deepest point with a sterile applicator).
  • Undermining and tunneling: use the clock face with 12 o'clock toward the head, for example "undermining 2.0 cm from 3 to 6 o'clock."
  • Photograph consistently: same distance and angle, with a measuring guide, following consent and privacy policies.
  • Number wounds the same way on every note. When one heals, document the date and final appearance before renumbering.

Lesson 2 of 3

Documentation that protects you

Document the barriers to healing and what you did about each one:

  • Support surface, heel protection, turning schedule, and any refusals or nonadherence (with education given)
  • Nutrition: intake, weights, dietitian involvement, supplements, relevant labs
  • Perfusion: pulses, ABI, vascular referrals and their results
  • Glycemic control, infection management, PT and OT, and other consults
  • Conversations with the patient and family about the diagnosis, plan and expected timeline

In facilities: do and document a full skin assessment on admission and readmission. Federal nursing home rules (F686) require care that prevents avoidable pressure injuries, and injuries first found after admission are generally counted as facility-acquired, so early documentation of present-on-admission wounds matters for the patient and the facility.

Partner with facilities on prevention programs. Most referrals come after wounds are complex, but prevention has the greatest impact and lowers costs. Keep patient outcomes first when balancing cost.

Use modifiers (for example 59 or the X modifiers, and 25 for a separate E/M on a procedure day) only when documentation supports a distinct service. Debridement is billed by the depth and area of tissue removed, not the wound's stage. Coding rules, NCCI edits and local coverage determinations change every year; verify with current CPT, your Medicare contractor and each payer.

Clinical pearls

  • Measure the same way every time: head-to-toe length, perpendicular width, deepest depth.
  • Document the barriers to healing and your action on each one.
  • Bill debridement by the deepest tissue actually removed, and say so in the note.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • A wound not documented on admission to a facility: assess and document it the same day.
  • Weeks of identical notes with no change in size or plan: reassess the wound and the plan.
  • Billing a deeper debridement than the note supports: correct before submitting.

Case

You sharply debride slough and some subcutaneous fat from a 6 cm x 4 cm wound, then apply a multilayer wrap to the same leg.

Show discussion

The debrided area is 24 sq cm with subcutaneous tissue removed, so 11042 plus 11045 for the extra 4 sq cm. Note the depth, tissue type and instrument. The multilayer wrap on the same leg on the same day is usually bundled with the debridement under NCCI edits, so check the edit before billing 29581.

Patient handout · plain language

Questions to ask about your wound care

  • What kind of wound is this, and what caused it?
  • Is it getting smaller? Can I see the measurements?
  • What can I do at home to help it heal?
  • How long do you expect healing to take?
  • What signs mean I should call you?
  • Will my insurance cover the dressings or treatments?

It's normal for chronic wounds to take weeks or months. Ask us any time you're unsure about your care.
