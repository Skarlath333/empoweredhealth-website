Lesson 1 of 4

Pressure injuries

Pressure injuries form over bony prominences from sustained pressure, often with shear (skin stays put while deeper tissue slides, as when a patient slides down in bed). Friction alone causes superficial damage and is not a pressure injury, but it adds to shear.

StageNPIAP description
Stage 1Intact skin with non-blanchable redness. In darker skin, look for color change, warmth, firmness or pain.
Stage 2Partial-thickness loss into the dermis: a pink or red shallow wound or an intact or ruptured serum-filled blister. No slough, eschar or granulation.
Stage 3Full-thickness skin loss into fat. Granulation and rolled edges may be present; slough or eschar may be present but don't hide the depth; undermining and tunneling possible.
Stage 4Full-thickness loss exposing fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage or bone.
UnstageableFull-thickness loss whose base is obscured by slough or eschar, so depth can't be seen.
Deep tissue pressure injuryIntact or non-intact skin with persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon or purple color, or a blood-filled blister. Can evolve quickly.

Also know medical device-related pressure injuries (under tubing, braces, oxygen cannulas, compression devices) and Kennedy terminal ulcers: sudden butterfly or pear-shaped sacral wounds at the end of life that may not be preventable. Goals shift to comfort, odor and drainage control.

Lesson 3 of 4

Moisture damage and traumatic wounds

Moisture-associated skin damage (MASD), including incontinence-associated dermatitis, comes from urine, stool, sweat or wound drainage. Skin is red, shiny, macerated or eroded, often with diffuse edges. It isn't a pressure injury, but it often leads to one on the sacrum and buttocks.

Lesson 4 of 4

Burns and other wounds

Burn depthAppearancePain
Superficial (first degree)Red, dry, no blistersPainful
Superficial partial thicknessPink, moist, blisters, blanchesVery painful
Deep partial thicknessMottled red and white, less blanchingLess painful
Full thickness (third degree)White, leathery or charred, dryOften painless

Refer to a burn center (American Burn Association criteria) for partial-thickness burns over 10% of body surface area, any full-thickness burn, burns of the face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major joints, electrical, chemical or inhalation injuries, and burns in patients with significant comorbidities or in children without appropriate care. Estimate area with the Rule of Nines or a Lund-Browder chart.

Other wounds: surgical incisions (watch for infection and dehiscence), osteomyelitis (bone infection, often under diabetic foot ulcers and stage 4 pressure injuries; the wound won't heal until it's treated), fistulas and abscesses, malignant wounds, and wounds from autoimmune disease such as pyoderma gangrenosum or vasculitis. Biopsy atypical or non-healing wounds.

Clinical pearls

  • If you can't see the wound base because of slough or eschar, it's unstageable. Don't guess the stage.
  • Pain at night that eases when the leg hangs down points to arterial disease.
  • A wound in an unusual spot or with an unusual look that won't heal deserves a biopsy.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Cold, pale, pulseless foot or new black toes: urgent vascular evaluation.
  • Burns meeting burn center criteria, or any inhalation, electrical or chemical burn.
  • Rapidly spreading purple discoloration, crepitus or pain out of proportion: possible necrotizing infection, emergency care.

Case

A 78-year-old with diabetes has a shallow, painless, round ulcer under the first metatarsal head with a thick callus rim. Pedal pulses are faint.

Show discussion

This is a diabetic (neuropathic) foot ulcer, possibly with PAD given faint pulses. Offload the area, debride the callus, check an ABI (and toe pressures if vessels may be non-compressible), probe to bone and consider an X-ray to look for osteomyelitis, and refer to vascular if perfusion is poor.

Patient handout · plain language

Pressure injuries (bedsores)

Pressure injuries happen when skin is pressed between a bone and a bed or chair for too long. They often form on the tailbone, hips, heels and elbows.

  • Watch for: red or dark skin that doesn't fade when pressed, blisters, or open spots.
  • Move often. Change position at least every 2 hours in bed, and shift weight every 15 to 30 minutes in a chair.
  • Float the heels on a pillow under the calves so heels don't touch the bed.
  • Keep skin clean and dry, and use a barrier cream if there's leakage of urine or stool.

Call us if you see a new sore, dark purple skin, or a sore that's getting bigger.
