Lesson 2 of 4

Venous and arterial ulcers

Venous ulcers: compression is the treatment. Confirm arterial flow first (ABI).

  • ABI 0.8 or higher: full compression (about 40 mmHg at the ankle) with multilayer systems or an Unna boot.
  • ABI 0.5 to 0.8: reduced compression only, with vascular input.
  • ABI under 0.5, or uncontrolled heart failure: no compression.

Practical sequence: antimicrobial foam or silver alginate on the wound, absorbent pad, then wrap from the toes to just below the knee. Change daily or three times a week. Use an Unna boot (zinc paste gauze, then rolled gauze and an elastic or cohesive wrap) for weeping legs. After 1 to 2 weeks, step up to multilayer compression; once healed, fit 20 to 40 mmHg stockings for life to prevent recurrence. Coordinate diuretics for fluid overload with the patient's primary clinician. Refer to vascular for venous ablation, which speeds healing and reduces recurrence (EVRA trial).

Arterial ulcers: perfusion first. Refer to vascular surgery for ABI under 0.9 with a wound. Locally, paint with povidone-iodine to keep a dry, stable eschar and prevent infection until blood flow is restored. Trim eschar edges only as they lift. Don't compress, soak, or aggressively debride an ischemic wound. Necrotic toes: keep dry and involve vascular early.

Lesson 3 of 4

Diabetic foot ulcers

Offloading is the cornerstone. No dressing heals a wound that's still being walked on.

StepWhat to do
OffloadTotal contact cast or a non-removable knee-high walker is best for plantar forefoot ulcers; removable walkers or custom shoes otherwise
DebrideSharp debridement of callus and slough, usually weekly
PerfusionABI and toe pressures; vascular referral if inadequate
InfectionAssess every visit; probe to bone and X-ray if deep (see Module 7)
Local careClean, then collagen with an antimicrobial foam or alginate based on drainage; change daily to three times a week
SystemicGlycemic control, smoking cessation, nutrition

When standard care fails to shrink the wound by about 40 to 50% in 4 weeks, consider advanced therapies (Module 6) and recheck perfusion and infection.

Lesson 4 of 4

Skin tears, abrasions and minor burns

Skin tears: clean gently, realign the flap with a moist cotton swab, and secure with skin closure strips or a silicone contact layer (no sutures in fragile skin). Cover with petrolatum gauze or silicone foam, and mark an arrow showing which way to remove the dressing so the flap isn't lifted. Change two to three times a week. Once the flap adheres, protect with barrier film. Pad bed rails and wheelchairs and moisturize skin twice daily.

Abrasions: irrigate with saline. Dry abrasions: povidone-iodine to form a protective scab. Wet abrasions: petrolatum gauze or zinc barrier, with foam if needed.

Minor burns (superficial and small partial thickness): cool with running water for about 20 minutes, don't pop intact blisters, and cover with a nonadherent or antimicrobial dressing. Silver sulfadiazine is still used but can slow epithelialization; avoid it on the face, in sulfa allergy and in pregnancy near term. Refer per burn center criteria (Module 2).

Clinical pearls

  • Compression heals venous ulcers. Teach patients the wraps feel tight at first and get easier as swelling falls.
  • In arterial wounds, dry stable eschar is your friend until blood flow is fixed.
  • Mark skin-tear dressings with an arrow so the next person removes them the right way.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Compression causing numbness, color change or new pain: remove it and recheck perfusion.
  • A diabetic foot that is hot, red and swollen without a wound: consider acute Charcot foot; offload and refer.
  • Necrotic toes, rest pain, or a non-healing ischemic wound: vascular surgery now.

Case

An 82-year-old has a weeping venous ulcer with 3+ pitting edema. ABI is 0.95 in both legs. She has stable heart failure.

Show discussion

Arterial flow is adequate, so compression is safe; coordinate with her cardiology or primary clinician given heart failure. Use silver alginate or antimicrobial foam, an absorbent pad, and an Unna boot with rolled gauze and an elastic wrap, changed three times a week. Elevate legs, review diuretics, step up to multilayer compression as weeping settles, then fit stockings after healing and refer for venous evaluation.

Patient handout · plain language

Leg and foot wounds

If you have swollen legs and a venous wound:

  • Wear your wraps or stockings as directed. They feel tight at first and get easier as swelling goes down.
  • Raise your legs above your heart for 30 minutes a few times a day.
  • Walk if you can. Calf muscles help push blood back up.

If you have diabetes and a foot wound:

  • Keep weight off the wound. Wear the boot or shoe we give you every time you stand or walk, even at home.
  • Check both feet every day with a mirror or a helper.
  • Keep your blood sugar in your target range.

Take your wraps off and call us if your toes become numb, cold, blue or more painful.
