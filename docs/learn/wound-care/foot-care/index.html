Lesson 1 of 3

The foot exam and who needs foot care

Foot care keeps feet and nails healthy, prevents injury and infection, and often brings immediate relief. Most patients are older adults, people with diabetes, PAD or neuropathy, and people who can't reach or see their feet. Ingrown toenails also affect younger, active people.

Exam: look for ulcers, deformity, redness, swelling, drainage, fissures between toes, callus and nail changes; palpate for pain and heel bogginess; check pulses, temperature and edema. Perform the 10 g monofilament test (plus vibration or pinprick) at least yearly in diabetes, more often in high-risk feet.

Key terms: onychomycosis (fungal nail), hypertrophic nails, paronychia (infected nail fold), corns and calluses, fissures, plantar warts, bunions, and gout (sudden red, hot, painful joint, often the big toe).

Prevention includes regular exams, nail and callus care, offloading, proper footwear, diabetes control, and teaching patients and caregivers to check feet daily.

Lesson 2 of 3

Nails, ingrown nails and calluses

Get consent first. Position the foot so both you and the patient are comfortable.

  • Soaking: a warm soak softens nails, but don't soak patients with diabetes and neuropathy, significant PAD, or open wounds; use moisture-only cleansing instead.
  • Nail trimming and debridement: trim in small bites, roughly straight across, without cutting into skin; watch for curved "horseshoe" nails and protruding skin. Reduce thickened nails with a rotary burr, using dust extraction and eye protection. File sharp edges.
  • Simple ingrown nail: with clean technique, cut a narrow vertical strip of the offending nail edge back toward the cuticle and lift it out. Many patients tolerate this without anesthesia if done slowly; use a digital block when needed. Use silver nitrate for bleeding (rinse with saline afterward to limit stinging). Refer for partial nail avulsion with matrixectomy for recurrent or infected ingrown nails.
  • Callus and corn paring: pare layer by layer with a scalpel or burr until the tissue softens, pausing to avoid heat. Never pare to bleeding. Address the cause with padding, footwear or orthotics.
  • Aftercare: clean, moisturize the skin but not between the toes, and dress any break in the skin (povidone-iodine or mupirocin for a week). Teach signs of infection.

Documentation: record the exam (pulses, sensation, skin, nails), the class findings that establish medical necessity for routine foot care under Medicare (for example diabetes with neuropathy or PAD, documented with the treating physician when required), the number of nails debrided, lesions pared, and the plan. Common diagnoses include onychomycosis, hypertrophic nails, PAD or atherosclerosis, diabetes with or without neuropathy, difficulty walking, foot pain, hyperkeratosis and ingrown nail. Typical follow-up for routine nail care is every 9 to 10 weeks or as covered; see patients sooner for ingrown nails or calluses as needed.

Common codes (verify current rules): 11720 and 11721 (nail debridement, 1 to 5 and 6 or more nails), 11055 to 11057 (paring of corns and calluses by number), 11730 (nail avulsion), and 11750 (nail excision with matrixectomy), with Q modifiers for Medicare class findings.

Clinical pearls

  • Don't soak the feet of patients with diabetic neuropathy or severe PAD.
  • Take your time trimming. Watch for skin that curves up into the nail line.
  • Every foot visit is a chance to teach daily foot checks and proper shoes.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • A hot, red, swollen foot in a patient with neuropathy: consider Charcot foot or infection; offload and refer.
  • Paronychia with spreading redness, or any foot wound in a patient with diabetes: treat promptly and refer to podiatry as needed.
  • Dark streaks in a nail or a changing pigmented lesion: refer for evaluation of melanoma.

Case

A 74-year-old with diabetes and neuropathy has thick, yellow, painful toenails and a callus under the second metatarsal head. Pulses are faint.

Show discussion

Skip the soak. Check an ABI given faint pulses. Debride the nails with a burr and pare the callus without drawing blood, then pad and adjust footwear to offload the metatarsal head. Document neuropathy and PAD findings that support medical necessity. Discuss onychomycosis treatment options honestly, and teach daily foot checks. Schedule routine care about every 9 to 10 weeks.

Patient handout · plain language

Caring for your feet

  • Look at your feet every day, including the bottoms and between your toes. Use a mirror or ask for help.
  • Wash and dry your feet daily, especially between the toes. Moisturize the tops and bottoms, but not between the toes.
  • Wear shoes and socks that fit, even indoors. Check inside shoes before putting them on.
  • Don't cut corns or calluses yourself or use acid corn removers.
  • If you have diabetes, don't soak your feet or test water with your feet. Use your hand or elbow.

Call us for a sore, blister or cut that isn't healing, redness or swelling, a toenail digging into the skin, or a foot that's suddenly hot or misshapen.
