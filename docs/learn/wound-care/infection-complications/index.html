Lesson 1 of 3

Recognizing infection

All chronic wounds carry bacteria. Treat infection, not colonization.

LevelSignsAction
Local infectionTwo or more of: warmth, redness or swelling over 2 cm beyond the edge (diabetic foot: 0.5 to 2 cm is mild), new or increased pain, purulence, new odor after cleansing, friable tissue, stalled healingCulture (Levine or tissue), topical antimicrobials, oral antibiotics, debride
Deep or spreadingRedness beyond 2 cm, lymphangitis, abscess, deep tissue or bone involvementOral or IV antibiotics; imaging; surgical consult for drainage
SystemicFever or low temperature, chills, fast heart rate or breathing, low blood pressure, confusion, high white countEmergency evaluation, blood cultures, IV antibiotics, admission

Neuropathy, PAD, steroids and older age blunt the signs. A diabetic foot infection may show only rising glucose or a new smell.

Lesson 2 of 3

Antibiotics and osteomyelitis

SettingCommon optionsNotes
Outpatient, mild to moderateCephalexin or amoxicillin-clavulanate; doxycycline or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole when MRSA is a concern; clindamycin as an alternativeUsually 1 to 2 weeks for soft tissue; check kidney function and interactions (TMP-SMX with warfarin and ACE inhibitors)
Inpatient, moderate to severeVancomycin plus cefepime or piperacillin-tazobactam, with metronidazole when anaerobes are likelyNarrow to culture results; reserve carbapenems for resistant organisms

Osteomyelitis: confirm with MRI and, ideally, bone biopsy for culture before long-term antibiotics. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause. Involve infectious disease and podiatry or surgery. About 6 weeks of therapy is typical when no infected bone is removed; IWGDF/IDSA 2023 supports oral agents with good bone penetration in many cases, and much shorter courses after complete resection: a few days with clean bone margins, or about 3 weeks if infected bone remains at the margin. Surgical removal of dead bone is often needed.

Antibiotics treat infection; they don't replace debridement, drainage, offloading or revascularization.

Lesson 3 of 3

Stalled wounds, complications and referrals

ProblemLikely causesWhat to do
Stalled woundPressure, poor nutrition, weight loss, infection, biofilm, ischemia, edema, wrong diagnosisReassess everything; consider biopsy; change approach after 2 to 4 weeks without progress
Epibole (rolled edges)Edges curl under and stop migratingOpen the edges: sharp debridement or silver nitrate, then moist healing
OdorInfection, necrotic tissue, anaerobesDebride, cleanse with Dakin's, charcoal or cadexomer iodine dressings, topical metronidazole (off-label)
AnemiaIron, B12 or folate deficiency; chronic diseaseIdentify and treat the cause
Surgical dehiscenceInfection, tension, poor nutritionContact the surgeon; close follow-up
HypergranulationExcess moisture, frictionReduce moisture; foam; silver nitrate if needed

Refer early: vascular surgery (abnormal ABI, ischemic wounds), infectious disease or the hospital (systemic infection, osteomyelitis, failed oral therapy), general or plastic surgery (large debridement, flaps, exposed hardware), and podiatry (foot and ankle wounds, Charcot foot, surgical offloading). Build relationships with good home health agencies and dietitians too.

Be cautious with any plan that debrides aggressively at every visit without measurable progress. The goal is healing, not ongoing treatment; get a second opinion when wounds stall for months.

Clinical pearls

  • Culture when you see infection, not to "see what grows."
  • Rising glucose in a diabetic patient with a foot wound can be the first sign of infection.
  • When you talk with families, overestimate healing time rather than underestimate it.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Fever, low blood pressure, confusion or rapidly spreading redness: emergency care.
  • Crepitus, gas on X-ray, or pain out of proportion: possible necrotizing infection; emergency surgery.
  • Exposed bone or positive probe-to-bone: osteomyelitis workup and specialist referral.

Case

A patient with a stage 4 ischial wound has a new foul odor, 3 cm of surrounding redness and warmth, and purulent drainage, but no fever and normal vital signs.

Show discussion

This is a local, possibly deep, infection. Get a Levine or tissue culture after cleansing, debride necrotic tissue, start an oral agent covering staph and strep (and anaerobes if odor and necrosis suggest them), and use antimicrobial dressings. Because it's a stage 4 wound over bone, evaluate for osteomyelitis with ESR, CRP and MRI. Escalate if systemic signs appear.

Patient handout · plain language

Signs of wound infection

Call us the same day if you notice:

  • Redness or warmth spreading around the wound
  • More pain, swelling or drainage
  • Pus, or a bad smell that stays after cleaning
  • The wound getting bigger or deeper
  • Higher blood sugars than usual if you have diabetes

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for fever with chills, red streaks moving up the arm or leg, confusion, fast heartbeat, or feeling very sick.

If you're given antibiotics, take every dose, and keep doing your wound care.
