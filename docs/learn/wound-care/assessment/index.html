Lesson 1 of 3

History and skin assessment

Many wounds look alike but have different causes. The history usually tells you which: diabetes, vascular disease, smoking, steroids, recent surgery, mobility, continence, intake and weight trends, and how the wound started.

Head-to-toe skin assessment at every new visit:

  • All bony prominences: sacrum, coccyx, ischia, hips, heels, ankles, elbows, occiput, ears
  • Skin folds, under the breasts and abdomen, and the perineum
  • Under every device: oxygen tubing, braces, catheters, compression devices, NPWT tubing
  • Legs and feet: hair loss, color, temperature, pulses, edema, staining
  • Where and how the patient lives: bed- or chair-bound, support surfaces, who helps them

In darker skin, redness is hard to see. Use temperature, firmness, swelling and pain. Check blanching: redness that blanches is reactive; non-blanchable redness is a stage 1 injury.

Tissue types: estimate the percentage of each in the wound bed (for example 60% granulation, 30% slough, 10% epithelial): dermal, granulation, slough, unstable eschar, stable eschar, epithelial tissue, and skin flaps.

Lesson 2 of 3

Labs and wound cultures

LabWhy
CBCInfection, anemia (poor oxygen delivery), platelets before debridement
CMPKidney function (protein and drug dosing), glucose, albumin
A1cDiabetes and glycemic control
CRP and ESRInflammation; a very high ESR raises concern for osteomyelitis
Iron studies, B12, folateWhen anemia is present
Vitamin D, zincWhen deficiency is suspected
PT/INRAnticoagulated patients, before sharp debridement

Albumin and prealbumin fall with inflammation and illness, so they are markers of severity and risk rather than precise measures of nutrition. Pair them with intake and weight.

Culture only when infection is suspected (increased pain, spreading redness, warmth, purulence, odor, sudden deterioration, or stalled healing), not on every chronic wound. Use the Levine technique: cleanse with saline, don't swab necrotic tissue or old drainage, then rotate a swab over 1 cm2 of viable tissue for 5 seconds with enough pressure to express fluid. Tissue samples after debridement are better still.

Lesson 3 of 3

Ankle-brachial index and imaging

How to perform an ABI: rest the patient supine for 5 to 10 minutes. Measure systolic pressure with a handheld Doppler in both arms (brachial), then at each ankle using the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries. For each leg, divide the higher ankle pressure by the higher arm pressure.

ABIMeaningAction
Over 1.40Non-compressible, calcified vessels (common in diabetes and kidney disease)Get toe pressures or toe-brachial index and arterial duplex
1.00 to 1.40NormalCompression generally safe
0.91 to 0.99BorderlineTreat risk factors; monitor
0.50 to 0.90PAD (mild to moderate)Vascular referral; reduced compression only with care
Under 0.50Severe PADUrgent vascular referral; no compression

Osteomyelitis workup: probe to bone, ESR and CRP, and plain X-rays first (they may be normal early). MRI is the best imaging test. Nuclear medicine scans are an option when MRI isn't possible. Bone biopsy with culture and histology is the reference standard and guides antibiotics.

Clinical pearls

  • Look under every device during every skin check.
  • Don't culture a wound that doesn't look infected. You'll grow colonizers and treat the wrong thing.
  • An ABI over 1.40 is not reassuring. It means you need toe pressures.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • ABI under 0.50, rest pain or gangrene: urgent vascular referral.
  • Probe-to-bone positive wound or bone exposed: work up for osteomyelitis.
  • A wound with systemic signs of infection: same-day evaluation.

Case

A 70-year-old smoker has a painful, dry, punched-out ulcer on the lateral ankle and a mild venous-looking edema. You plan to start compression. Her ABI is 0.62.

Show discussion

Her ABI shows moderate PAD, so standard high compression is unsafe. Refer to vascular surgery, keep the wound dry and protected while perfusion is assessed, and use only reduced, carefully monitored compression if vascular agrees. Strongly support smoking cessation.

Patient handout · plain language

What to expect at your wound visit

  • We'll ask how the wound started and about your health, medicines and diet.
  • We'll check your skin from head to toe, including your heels and backside.
  • We'll measure the wound and may take a photo to track healing.
  • For leg or foot wounds, we may check blood pressure at your ankles to make sure enough blood is reaching the wound.
  • We may order blood work, a culture (a swab of the wound), or an X-ray or MRI.

Bring to your visit: a list of your medicines and supplements, and the names of the dressings you've been using.
