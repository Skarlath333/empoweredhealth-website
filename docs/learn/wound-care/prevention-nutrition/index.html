Lesson 1 of 3

Pressure relief and support surfaces

Consistent pressure relief matters more than any dressing. Highest-risk sites: sacrum and coccyx, ischial tuberosities, buttocks, heels, hips, elbows, ankles, and the plantar foot in diabetes.

  • Turn bed-bound patients at least every 2 hours (individualize to skin response and surface), using a 30-degree side-lying tilt and a lift sheet to avoid shear.
  • Shift weight in a chair every 15 to 30 minutes, and limit sitting time for patients with sacral or ischial wounds.
  • Keep the head of the bed at 30 degrees or lower when possible to reduce sliding.
  • Watch body mechanics: patients who always lie on one side or stack their feet need targeted padding.

Lesson 2 of 3

Heels, devices, skin and continence

  • Float the heels: pillows under the calves or heel-suspension boots so the heels don't touch the bed. Apply barrier film to at-risk heels.
  • Devices: check under oxygen tubing, braces, compression devices, catheters, restraints and NPWT tubing every shift. Pad with silicone foam or moleskin, and remove objects left in beds and chairs.
  • Prophylactic dressings: multilayer silicone foam over the sacrum and heels lowers pressure injury rates in high-risk patients.
  • Skin care: use pH-balanced cleansers (skin's natural pH is about 4.5 to 5.5), avoid harsh soaps and hot water, and moisturize daily.
  • Incontinence: check and change promptly, cleanse gently, apply a zinc or dimethicone barrier, treat diarrhea and constipation, and look for reversible causes. Consider a fecal management system for persistent liquid stool. Use indwelling catheters only when clearly indicated.
  • Falls: clear rugs and clutter, add rails, manage toileting needs, and refer to PT and OT for strength and transfers.

Weigh weekly. Unintended weight loss of 5% in a month or 10% in 6 months signals malnutrition and slows healing. Track meal percentages and offer protein supplements between meals. Refer to a dietitian early for large or complex wounds, and document nutrition interventions: it supports the care plan and explains slow healing.

Smoking constricts vessels and slows healing. Offer cessation support at every visit.

Clinical pearls

  • No cushion replaces repositioning, and no dressing replaces offloading.
  • Float the heels. Complete offloading is the best heel protection.
  • Weekly weights catch decline before the wound does.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Weight loss over 5% in a month or eating less than half of meals: dietitian referral and supplements now.
  • Swallowing problems or coughing with meals: speech therapy evaluation.
  • New pressure injury despite a prevention plan: reassess the surface, turning schedule and nutrition.

Case

A 68 kg resident with a stage 3 sacral wound eats half of each meal and has lost 4 kg in a month. Kidney function is normal.

Show discussion

She has lost about 6% of her weight in a month, which is significant. Aim for about 2,000 to 2,400 kcal and 85 to 100 g protein a day; add a high-protein or arginine-containing supplement between meals, involve a dietitian, check for depression, dental or swallowing problems, and weigh weekly. Pair this with an alternating pressure mattress and limited sitting time.

Patient handout · plain language

Preventing pressure injuries and eating to heal

  • Change position at least every 2 hours in bed, and shift your weight every 15 to 30 minutes in a chair.
  • Keep heels off the bed with a pillow under the calves.
  • Keep skin clean and dry. Use a gentle, pH-balanced cleanser and a barrier cream if there's leakage.
  • Eat protein at every meal, plus a protein shake between meals if you're not eating well.
  • Drink water through the day unless your doctor limits fluids.
  • Weigh yourself once a week. Tell us if you're losing weight without trying.

Caregivers: look at the skin on the tailbone, hips and heels every day.
