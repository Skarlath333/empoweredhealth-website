Lesson 1 of 3

The wound care role

Wound care is the assessment, treatment and prevention of acute and chronic wounds: damage to skin and the tissue beneath it from pressure, trauma, poor circulation, diabetes, infection, cancer, radiation and other causes. An estimated 7 million or more Americans live with a chronic wound, and Medicare alone spends tens of billions of dollars a year on them. Demand is rising with age, diabetes, obesity and vascular disease.

The goal is not the dressing. The wound care provider treats the wound and the reasons it formed or won't heal:

  • Relieve pressure and offload
  • Optimize protein, calories, vitamins and hydration
  • Manage diabetes, vascular disease, edema and other comorbidities
  • Treat the whole person, including pain, mood and isolation

Care can happen almost anywhere: clinics, skilled nursing and assisted living facilities, home visits, and hospitals. Wounds also take a heavy toll beyond the skin. Pain, odor and drainage lead many patients to withdraw socially, so ask about mood and daily life.

Lesson 2 of 3

Scope, liability and who is at risk

Scope. Basic wound care (assessment, dressing selection, education) is within every NP's scope. Advanced procedures such as conservative sharp debridement, biopsy, incision and drainage, and skin substitute application depend on state rules, collaboration agreements, facility privileges and training. Certifications such as CWCN-AP and WCS-C deepen skills and credibility.

Liability. Wound care is a moderate-risk specialty. Carry your own malpractice policy if you own a practice or work in facilities or homes. The most common problems are:

RiskHow to reduce it
Incomplete documentationMeasure, photograph and describe every wound every visit; record rationale and response
Ignoring the causeDocument what you did about pressure, nutrition, glucose, perfusion and infection
Working outside scopeKnow your state board rules and facility privileges
Delayed referralRefer early for ischemia, deep infection, osteomyelitis or stalled wounds
Poor communicationDocument consent, education, and family updates

High-risk groups: older adults, the critically ill, hospice and palliative patients, people with spinal cord injury, the malnourished, infants, sedated patients, dialysis patients, people frequently transported by stretcher, and people with poor hygiene or self-care.

Lesson 3 of 3

How wounds heal, and why they stall

PhaseTimingWhat happens
HemostasisMinutesPlatelets and clotting factors form a fibrin clot that stops bleeding and makes a scaffold
InflammationAbout days 1 to 3 (longer in chronic wounds)Neutrophils kill bacteria; macrophages clear debris and release growth factors
ProliferationAbout days 4 to 24Granulation tissue, new vessels, contraction and epithelialization; beefy red granulation is a good sign
Remodeling3 weeks to 2 yearsCollagen reorganizes; scar reaches only about 80% of the original skin's strength

Chronic wounds get stuck in inflammation. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) normally clear damaged matrix, but in chronic wounds levels (especially MMP-2 and MMP-9) run too high. They break down new tissue and growth factors. Collagen and protease-modulating dressings help bind excess protease activity.

Key terms to know: granulation (red, cobblestone healthy tissue), slough (yellow to tan nonviable tissue), stable eschar (dry, hard, adherent) versus unstable eschar (soft, boggy, draining), epibole (rolled edges that stop epithelial migration), maceration (white, waterlogged skin), undermining and tunneling (measured in cm by clock position), and biofilm (invisible bacterial communities that stall healing).

Risk tools: the Braden Scale scores sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction and shear (6 to 23). Scores of 18 or less signal risk, and 12 or less is very high risk. Use it alongside, not instead of, clinical judgment.

Clinical pearls

  • Treat the person as a whole, not just the hole in the person.
  • If you didn't document it, it didn't happen. That applies to education and offloading as much as to measurements.
  • A wound stuck in inflammation needs its cause fixed, not just a stronger dressing.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Signs of systemic infection (fever, low blood pressure, confusion, rising white count): urgent evaluation.
  • Exposed bone, tendon or hardware: assume deep infection until proven otherwise and involve surgery.
  • A wound that hasn't shrunk in 2 to 4 weeks of good care: reassess the diagnosis, perfusion and infection.

Case

A new facility patient has a sacral wound that has been dressed with the same foam for two months without change. Her Braden score is 11, she eats about 30% of meals, and she sits up in a chair most of the day.

Show discussion

The dressing isn't the problem. She is at very high risk with poor intake and constant sitting pressure. Address offloading first (time in bed on a pressure-redistributing surface, chair limits), get a dietitian involved and add protein supplements, reassess the wound fully (measurements, tissue types, infection signs), and document each intervention.

Patient handout · plain language

Understanding your wound

Healing a wound takes more than a bandage. Your body needs:

  • Pressure off the wound. Change positions often, and keep weight off it.
  • Good food. Protein at every meal (eggs, meat, fish, beans, Greek yogurt, protein shakes).
  • Water, unless your doctor has limited your fluids.
  • Blood sugar control if you have diabetes.
  • No smoking. Nicotine cuts blood flow to the wound.

Chronic wounds can take weeks to months to heal. We'll measure your wound at each visit so you can see progress.

Get care right away for fever or chills, redness spreading from the wound, a bad new smell, or new confusion.
