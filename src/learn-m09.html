Exposure history and counseling

A 5-minute exposure history (based on the ATSDR CH2OP approach): Community (nearby industry, air quality), Home (age, water source, renovation, mold, radon test), Hobbies (stained glass, firearms, ceramics), Occupation, and Personal habits (smoking, vaping, diet).

Practical swaps to share with patients:

  • Don't heat food in plastic; use glass or stainless steel.
  • Filter drinking water when there's a concern. Look for NSF/ANSI 53 (lead) or 58 (reverse osmosis) certifications that list PFAS. Test private wells.
  • Ventilate when cooking, especially with gas stoves.
  • Wet-dust and vacuum with a HEPA filter; take shoes off at the door.
  • Choose low-mercury fish such as salmon, sardines, shrimp and canned light tuna; avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish and bigeye tuna.
  • Choose fragrance-free products where practical.

Tests to avoid: provoked (chelation challenge) urine heavy metal tests are not valid and are discouraged by medical toxicologists. Urine mycotoxin panels aren't validated for diagnosing illness. Focus on lowering exposure, and test blood lead or PFAS only when there's a specific reason.

Clinical pearls

  • Ask about home age, water source and hobbies. They find most significant exposures.
  • Recommend a radon test to every homeowner. Kits are cheap.
  • Give one or two swaps at a time so advice doesn't turn into fear.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Elevated blood lead in a child or pregnant patient: follow public health guidance and report as required.
  • Symptoms that cluster at work or in one building, or in several household members.
  • Suspected carbon monoxide exposure (headache and nausea in several people at home): emergency care.

Case

A couple trying to conceive asks how to "detox." They live in a 1960s house with well water and are renovating the kitchen. One of them eats swordfish weekly.

Show discussion

Skip detox products and provoked metal testing. Recommend testing the well water (including lead and, if local data suggest it, PFAS), a radon test, lead-safe renovation practices (pre-1978 paint), and switching to low-mercury fish. Consider a blood lead level for the partner planning pregnancy given the renovation. Suggest glass food containers and ventilation while cooking.

Question 1: Which test is discouraged by medical toxicologists?

Question 2: At what radon level does EPA recommend fixing a home?

Patient handout · plain language

Simple swaps at home

You can't avoid every chemical, and you don't need to. A few simple steps lower the biggest exposures:

  • Test your home for radon. Kits are inexpensive.
  • Don't microwave food in plastic. Use glass or ceramic.
  • If you have well water, test it. Use a certified filter if needed.
  • Turn on the vent fan when you cook.
  • Take shoes off at the door, and dust with a damp cloth.
  • Pick lower-mercury fish like salmon, sardines and shrimp.

Living in a home built before 1978? Old paint may contain lead. Ask us before sanding or renovating, especially if you're pregnant or have young children.
