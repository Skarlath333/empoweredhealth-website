Lesson 1 of 3

Diagnosing testosterone deficiency

Diagnosis needs both symptoms (low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, low mood, loss of muscle) and low levels.

  • Test correctly: total testosterone in the morning (ideally fasting), on two separate days. The AUA uses under 300 ng/dL as a reasonable cutoff.
  • Free testosterone (by equilibrium dialysis or calculated) helps when SHBG is abnormal. Obesity and diabetes lower SHBG; aging and liver disease raise it.
  • Check LH to separate primary (testicular) from secondary (pituitary) causes. If LH is low or normal, check prolactin; consider iron studies and pituitary imaging when testosterone is very low.
  • Look for reversible causes: obesity, sleep apnea, opioids, glucocorticoids, heavy alcohol use, and acute illness. Weight loss alone often raises testosterone.
Lesson 2 of 3

Treatment and fertility

Ask about fertility first. Testosterone therapy suppresses sperm production and can cause infertility. Men who want children soon should not use it; specialists may use hCG or (off-label) clomiphene instead.

FormNotes
Gels and solutionsSteady levels; risk of transfer to women and children (wash hands, cover the site)
Injections (cypionate or enanthate, IM or subcutaneous)Inexpensive; peaks and troughs; weekly dosing smooths levels
Oral testosterone undecanoateTaken with food; monitor blood pressure
PelletsLong-acting; can't be adjusted once placed

Don't start with breast or prostate cancer, a palpable prostate nodule or elevated PSA without urology evaluation, elevated hematocrit (around 50% or higher), untreated severe sleep apnea, uncontrolled heart failure, a heart attack or stroke within 6 months, or a near-term wish for fertility.

Cardiovascular safety: in the TRAVERSE trial (men 45 to 80 with cardiovascular risk), testosterone gel did not increase major cardiovascular events, but atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, and acute kidney injury were more common.

Testosterone is a Schedule III controlled substance. Follow current DEA telehealth rules and your state's requirements.

Reassess at 3 to 6 months. If symptoms haven't improved despite normal levels, testosterone probably wasn't the cause. Shift the focus back to sleep, mood, weight and vascular health.

Clinical pearls

  • Never diagnose low testosterone from one afternoon level.
  • Ask every man about fertility plans before starting testosterone.
  • Rising hematocrit on therapy is a reason to look for sleep apnea.

Red flags: refer or work up first

  • Very low testosterone with low LH, headaches, or visual changes: check prolactin and image the pituitary.
  • Hematocrit over 54% on therapy.
  • A palpable prostate nodule or rapidly rising PSA.
  • Leg swelling, chest pain, or shortness of breath on therapy.

Case

A 34-year-old with low energy has a total testosterone of 262 ng/dL drawn at 4 p.m. His BMI is 36, he snores, and he and his partner hope to have a baby next year.

Show discussion

Repeat a morning level and add LH and SHBG or free testosterone, since obesity lowers SHBG. Screen for sleep apnea. Exogenous testosterone would suppress sperm production, so it's a poor fit with his fertility plans. Prioritize weight loss and sleep apnea treatment, which can raise testosterone, and refer to urology or endocrinology if treatment is still needed.

Patient handout · plain language

Low testosterone

Low testosterone can cause low sex drive, erection problems, tiredness, and low mood. But these symptoms have many causes, so we test carefully.

  • Testing: blood is drawn in the morning, on two different days.
  • Things that lower testosterone: extra weight, poor sleep or sleep apnea, some pain medicines, and heavy drinking. Fixing these can help on their own.
  • Want children? Tell us. Testosterone treatment can stop sperm production.
  • If you start treatment, you'll need regular blood tests to check your red blood cell count and PSA.
  • Using gel? Wash your hands and cover the area so it doesn't rub off on partners or children.
